## Report

### Time is rushing

Sometimes one has a feeling that either something will happen now or never. Twenty years ago I was
crossing the path of the famous West Highland Way (WHW) while sitting in the bus on a road trip
around Scotland. My mum pointed out that one day it would be great to walk WHW and enjoy the
Scottish hills and glens from a walking perspective. Since that time I have visited Scotland many
times, including my walk through the northern part of WHW in 2018, and five years later, Bee and I hiked in
the north-south direction from Fort William to Milngavie. This took us six days in total and we have
great memories of it. Each of these experiences was totally different due to weather circumstances,
but every time it was absolutely great. The uniqueness of this particular long distance path is in
combining several very different landscapes containing village and suburb areas, historical sights
of historical battles and dismantled monasteries, and highland sceneries at Trossachs Natural Park
in the Loch Lomond district and northern highlands. It is this particular feature of WHW that every
day gives a new perspective on the Scottish landscape, which results in a feeling of walking six
different tracks combined in one. Relatively low difficulty and good facility equipment around the
path made me wonder if this experience could be shared with older family members to fulfill an old
vision. And exactly this brought me to this year's expedition.

Planning such a journey, especially if not undertaken with well-experienced hikers, required from my
side a significant amount of preparation and planning of "what if". On the other hand, I also had a
few necessary conditions to make the journey more exciting for Bee and me. Both my previous visits
to WHW took the north to south direction, which is the opposite from the recommended one. Therefore,
I had an idea that if I ever walked WHW again I would prefer to do it by taking the south to north
direction. This means starting in Milngavie north of Glasgow, strolling through the Uplands towards
Loch Lomond followed by the valley of Tyndrum further to Glencoe moors, and finally spending the
last two days in Scottish mountains crossing Devil's Staircase and the valley of Allt na Lairige Mor
towards Fort William. Based on my previous experience I was able to quickly build up a plan for a
seven to eight day walk with the possibility of adaptation to weather and other circumstances. This
included packing the minimal necessary equipment for sleeping in tents in private camps and
protected natural campsites and one-day resources of water and food. Of course, as I expected, my
advice was taken only partly into account, and thus I also booked one-rucksack baggage transfer to
make the journey more enjoyable. Altogether, I converged to a rough plan for every day, and to the
decision to manage the precise plan through the walk day-by-day (with an expected significant amount
of improvisation). Thus we were able to start our journey in mid-July.

### Walk through West Highland Way


#### First day

The first day from Milngavie to Drymen is a combination of flat and straight pedestrian paths and a
short tarmac road at the end. This normally would be a very comfortable day, but the weather made it
rather exhausting. Our journey began before noon at the obelisk, which marks the start of WHW.
Before that, we travelled from Edinburgh to Glasgow and took a train to Milngavie. It was sunny already at
Milngavie, however, the first two or three kilometers with a short climb went through a suburb park
and forest, which made it undetectable for us how warm it was. Then it follows around a lake,
crosses a road, turns right around old oaks and by a dusty path down to the valley. Next to the
last part there is one of the most iconic hills of the walk, called Dumgoyach. By its shape and
green forest on the top, slightly resembling the shape of an old toad or well-preserved tomb of old
kings, it absolutely peaks above the valley, although it has maybe only a few tens of meters.
Especially the view from the north makes it spectacular (Btw. next to it there are standing stones,
so the place was noticed by pictic or pre-pictic civilizations). Below the hill there is a crossroad
behind the bridge and turning left leads to the second part of the first day's walk.

The second part crosses the uplands through a valley on the old rail track changed to pedestrian
paths. The old Blane Valley railway, I think, was made to reach the rural area to the north from Glasgow
in the second half of the nineteenth century and it was dismantled in the fifties and sixties. There the warm
weather and sunshine dried us within a few kilometers, however, don't worry. There are at least two
coffee shops and one honesty box on the next six kilometers in case of need.

There we experienced, for the first time, apparently a new trend on WHW. I was surprised that we met
at least four cyclists. Maybe it is a new trend to undergo WHW on a bike, but I think it is not a
very clever one. Firstly, there are some of the most beautiful parts of the trail, which are not
possible to normally cycle through around Loch Lomond and Devil's Staircase. Secondly, some of the
roads, which can be used by gravel cyclists, are full of hiking people, which leads to a high risk
of collisions. Later it happened to us that we were almost hit by a cyclist. Finally, the difficulty
of WHW is not high, and thus if you want to spend less than six days, you can in principle run it
from start to end within 3-4 days without any extreme training.

The last section of the day is on tarmac and it finishes in a small town in front of the Highlands
called Drymen. However, two kilometers before the town, there is a campsite for backpackers, which
we decided to take for the night. Even the last few kilometers were for me very exciting by watching
the rural lodges and cottages next to fields with cows and long oak drives going to mystery houses
out of our sight. I planned the Drymen visit for the morning to have breakfast in a local
supermarket to start the second day.

#### Second day

Three kilometers on tarmac to Drymen followed by a climb to Conic Hill opened to us a view at Loch
Lomond and the Trossachs Natural Park. In the center of Drymen, there is a small town square with a few
benches and a water tap next to a supermarket and coffee shop. Both places offer a hot breakfast to
start a day, which we used and then started slowly climbing up the road above Drymen to climb Conic
Hill. This is another example of small hills in Scotland with a few hundred meters elevation, which
can make it difficult to summit by a steep climb. Nevertheless, climbing it from south to north is
significantly more comfortable than the other direction. The weather was the same as on the previous
day with blue sky, which we happily enjoyed through the scenery of Loch Lomond before climbing down
to Balmaha. This rather touristic village seems to be always crowded. Hundreds of cars and thousands
of people enjoying the summer day made us have a quick late lunch and continue to Sallochy campsite.

A general observation was that the number of people on WHW was roughly around 10-15 people per day
in one direction. At least these numbers of people correspond to those who we met regularly at the
start and end of the day. Of course, there are many people who go just for a part of the track, and
many of those who go in the opposite direction. All together, the path is not crowded, however, in
comparison to other long distance paths in Scotland, especially those in the north, where you meet
one to four people for the whole trail, it is not as lonely an experience. This was also one of the
reasons why this track is so great for my mum, who likes to chat with foreign people. Concerning the
various nationalities, most people come from Benelux countries, Germany, Czech Republic, France, and
the UK. This is especially a warning for those who wonder about spending time without their fellow
citizens.

The last part of the day's trail led us from Balmaha to Sallochy campsite in Trossachs Natural Park.
The route was zig-zag crossing over small forested hills with a few views at Loch Lomond and sandy
beaches full of people doing barbecues and enjoying swimming on the warm day. The campsite I
remembered from my previous experience with WHW, as a perfectly placed one before Rowardennan. Also,
I remembered stunning scenic views and that it is frequently booked ahead. Thus I took a risk and
booked it already in late May, with the selection of a pitch with "great views". Looking back this
was a mistake. It is cheap and the location is indeed great, however, the smelly toilets and gravel
pitches next to the lake make it not so great for camping. If one insists on booking it, I recommend
booking one of the pitches in the central area, further away from toilets. Nevertheless, the late
evening swim in the lake made up this hiccup before the critical third day on the route.

#### Third (critical) day

The trail on the edge of Loch Lomond is always mentioned as the hard one. It starts five kilometers
from Sallochy campsite, just behind Rowardennan, where one can buy breakfast in a restaurant and
local (expensive) shop. The route follows a gravel road around an honesty box, hostel, and Nature
Park Rangers' lodge, and one can either take the upper road (which I have never used, so I can't
provide any advice) or the lower path around Loch Lomond. It is roughly a ten-kilometer-long path to
Inversnaid. The views at the lake are indeed great, although sometimes it is distracting when crossing
stones and more difficult paths. However, for me the most stunning part is the wood close to the
bothy (i.e., hut for free sleeping) and old abandoned village. Years ago, I remember that I had
never ever before seen a green color like that. The drawn pictures from Rivendell and Lothlórien
from Lord of the Rings are still less imposing. This time it was stunning again, however, the dry
weather from the previous week(s) took its (slight) toll. Feeling a little bit dried from the walk
and focusing on the way, we very much enjoyed that in the middle of nowhere next to Cailness falls,
there was an open coffee bar a few kilometers in front of Inversnaid. This port on the eastern
bank of Loch Lomond allows one to bypass the part of the track we walked by taking a ferry from
Rowardennan. I would greatly recommend this for those who would like to lower the difficulty of the
day.

The next part of the trail around Loch Lomond is more difficult and can't be crossed by ferry.
Roughly seven kilometers start with four kilometers of path with a similar difficulty as before. And
then it is followed by two kilometers of quite difficult trail, especially for older people or those
with knee problems. There I was betrayed by my memories. Last time I walked it from the north to
south, and as we passed the difficult part in the morning with plenty of energy, I didn't remember
how careful one should be with a heavy rucksack to not break a leg or ankle. My companions needed
significant time to climb it, which made it difficult at the end also for me, because the north of
Loch Lomond is surrounded by moors with trillions of midges. With luck, we passed it and it was
already after five o'clock in the afternoon. Knowing that the pub in the camp could stop cooking
before we arrived, I decided to run the last five kilometers to the camp, while the others took a
slow pace to the Beinglas camp.

The critical part of the day in the end was not the stones on the path, but midges. After dinner, we
thought that the worst was behind us and went to build the tents for sleep. Already during the
building of the tents, we realized that this would be a rough night. Our clothing with long sleeves
and long pants showed itself to be not enough, and the net of our tent also seemed to be only partly
effective. In the end, nothing helped and we got tens of stings. As some of us are allergic it was
more painful and annoying than one would expect. Never before had I experienced so many of them
while I was in Scotland. There are a few recommendations on the internet, but practically, we were
looking for the next day and reaching Tyndrum, where I expected significantly lower numbers of
midges.

#### Fourth (resting) day

The next day, we had to hike up around the river Falloch and waterfalls of the same name to the
crossroads above Crianlarich and then down to St. Fillan monastery in Kirkton and further to Tyndrum.
I was a little bit afraid of the first part after the last day as I remembered that the hill above
Crianlarich is rather steep from the other side. However, my fear was not confirmed. The direction
up from the Loch Lomond side is slowly going uphill in such a slow pace that it makes it a rather
comfortable climb. The bigger problem seemed to be crossing the main road in the end. I am surprised
that such a well-known path with so many hundreds of people crossing the road does not even have any
touch traffic light. But focusing on the more positive moments of the path, I noticed that animals
and especially birds along the path are extremely used to people.

The domesticated wild birds are apparently spread over the whole WHW. It happened to us at least
five times at different places on the WHW that a bird just sat on our backpack or next to us at less
than one meter distance observing us and waiting for a snack. Below one of the photos shows my
favourite robin who was sitting next to me on the trail mark showing the WHW way just below the hill
above Crianlarich.

Finally, we reached the valley leading to the crossroads at Tyndrum. I always enjoy seeing the ruins
of St. Fillan monastery and neighbouring graveyard with medieval slabstones. It is a memorial to the
old fight between Columba's and Benedictine traditions in the Catholic Church, which is well known also
from the Bohemian lands. It is then followed by a comfortable walking path going to St. Fillan's
"pool" on the river Fillan. It is a rather interesting place used for healing people of various
physical and mental diseases in medieval times, and you can still put your feet into it for cooling
them down after hiking. Then we went around the place of battle of Dalrigh from Scottish
Independence Wars (this battle is, I think, also shown in the recent movie Outlaw King) and the lake
of the lost sword to Tyndrum. This town is great for shopping for necessary equipment for the second
half of the WHW walk, or just to have a nice dinner/breakfast before the next day.

#### Fifth day

The fifth day was full of improvisation. I decided to try a train connection between Tyndrum (Upper
Tyndrum, not the Lower Tyndrum station) and ride to the train stop in Bridge of Orchy. This saved us
a few kilometers walking next to the road. And although there are still some spectacular views on
the way, I thought that we might enjoy a rather slow walk in Glencoe valley. I am not sure that
I would recommend it to everyone walking, but for older walkers, if one section of WHW can be
bypassed without great regrets then it is this one.

Glencoe valley was as magnificent as expected. I never noticed before that in fact in the
north-south direction you are slowly descending down for around fifteen kilometers. However, going
in the opposite direction, although slightly slower, was still very enjoyable without sweating too
much. The problem, which we almost forgot from the days before, however, appeared again and we were
bitten by hundreds of midges. Nevertheless, compared to the Beinglas campsite, it was nothing.
Magnificent views at the lakes, moors, and hills were more than a payback for the pain of stings.

When we reached the oldest skiing resort in Glencoe with a neighbouring camp, I thought that it
would be my first time sleeping there, but I realized that I had already spent there a night with H.
and my mum twenty years ago. As usual, we met "our pack of companions" from the previous days and
had a short chat about the last day's adventure. This is typical for WHW, as most people take 6-8
days for the whole walk, you have a high probability that every night you will all gather in a
single camp. This might be either an annoying experience, or for single travellers a way to meet and
chat with someone and get advice for the following day.

#### Sixth day

Starting early in the morning, we had breakfast in the Glencoe Mountain resort and then went three
kilometers to Kingshouse hotel. This place once saved my life against a massive storm, so I always
stop by at least for a coffee or tea. And then we crossed the moors and field around the main road
and finally got below the Devil's Staircase, which is like a gate to walk up in the mountains.
Before that, you can admire the magnificent views at the Scottish Highlands only from the valleys.
The Devil's Staircase is a rather short but steep climb and we quickly reached the top. However,
this is always dangerous in Scottish mountains. Not every mountain or hill with a few hundred meters
is easy to summit, and some with almost a thousand meters are very comfortable. It always matters
from what altitude you start.

During the climb we already started to hear extreme noise from the opposite valley and when we
reached the top we realized where it was coming from. Two F-35 fighters were flying low, practically
below us, through the valley. It seemed to be training for low altitude flying in harsh conditions.
I could only imagine that similarly, more than eighty years ago, some of the Czech pilots were
testing their skills before or during the [Battle of Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Britain).

From Devil's Staircase to the final stop of the day, Kinlochleven, it is roughly ten kilometers with
the last two of them with a steep decline. Kinlochleven is again one of these perfect towns for the
evening on the WHW with one open pub, which makes the decision significantly easy, and a small
supermarket.

#### Seventh day (and one quarter of eighth day)

Finally, we started the last day of WHW. I was extremely looking forward to this part of the trail
due to its distant feeling from the rest of the world. The high mountains from all sides can easily
cut you off from the world's stress and make you feel extremely happy for nothing. And at the end,
there is a hill with the most iconic view from all the WHW. The place called Dun Deardail used to be
a fort inhabited by Picts or a pre-Pictic population and today you can only imagine how magnificent a
place for living it was. I was once standing at the top and lightning struck nearby, and on the same
day one of the tourists climbing the opposite Ben Nevis died in the storm. Most of the people just
omit this sight, as they are too tired and to reach it one needs to add another kilometer. But for me
it is the most epic view of WHW.

The walking down to Nevis valley was again a quick one and then we decided to take a camp there
instead of going directly to Fort William to finish our track the next day. The large camping site
is for backpackers and campers. Therefore, it was the first time from Balmaha when you meet again
crowds of people. Unfortunately, I didn't find any other better way how or where to spend a night.
The next morning we made a short one-hour walk to Fort William and took the picture at the
well-earned rest.

This is probably the last time I walked WHW for a long time. However, I feel that when I am sixty I
might walk through WHW once again for the last time.

### Route from Fort William via Inverness, Aberdeen, Dundee, and St. Andrews (in few words)

Our next plan was one of the shorter long-distance paths in Scotland, but we felt a little bit too exhausted
to do it after WHW. Thus we decided that we would do a week-long round trip around Scottish castles
and cities.

We started by Urquhart castle sitting on the bank of Loch Ness. This is a typical thing in the UK: there
is almost nothing to see from the castle, but they know how to sell the story in such a way that it
looks like something big. In any case, the landscape and views at the lake were indeed beautiful.
Using the bus connection we then came to Inverness. Although it was a short visit, we saw the castle
and had a nice lunch in the new restaurant. And then we decided to go to Aberdeen.

Aberdeen, known as the capital of oil industry in Scotland, was a town of huge differences. On one
side, we found one of the most beautiful botanical gardens and iconic lighthouses. On the other,
when we turned to the backstreets, we almost started vomiting from how dirty and disgusting it was.
Our hotel, by the way, was apparently an old-luxury Victorian hotel like from the Hercule Poirot
stories.

I remembered from my younger age that from time to time I conquered Dunnottar castle in a game
called Stronghold. Therefore, our next stop was this castle. We were as usual lucky with the weather
and I would recommend this castle to everyone who likes the Scottish history of marshals and
protestant purges of Catholics in Scotland.

The next stop, again a short one, was Dundee, where we were recommended to visit the McManus Museum
and I can very much recommend it also to others. Again, I have to say, I am very surprised how dirty
Scottish cities are, especially in comparison to villages and small towns. Then we found a beautiful
new campsite in Tayport called Larrick. This was a perfect spot for our one-day trip to St. Andrews
University and town. One of my friends would call St. Andrews a one-hour city based on its size, but
we spent a whole day there by strolling through streets, abbeys, the castle, beaches, and shops.

The last day of the vacation we spent in Edinburgh before flying back to HH and Prague.
Unfortunately, my backpack was lost in Amsterdam, so I had to wait one day for it. Thank God I had
all important things in my pockets.

## Recommendations

- WHW: Going from the north-to-south direction is more demanding than the south-to-north direction. The
  reason is the steepness of hills and placement of villages and other camping facilities.
- Areas with the frequent presence of [Highland midges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_midge) are mainly between Inversnaid (north of Loch Lomond) and
  Glencoe. Repellents are not very effective, take a **bright-colored** long-sleeved shirt with long
  trousers combined with high socks and a head net on top of a hat.
- Larrick campsite in Tayport is a great camping spot for the area around Dundee, St. Andrews,
  Falkland. A combination of car with bicycle and one-week camping there might be a perfect vacation.
